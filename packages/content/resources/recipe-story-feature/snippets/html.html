The Slow Sunday Ragù That Taught Me to Wait

Six hours, one battered pot, and a sauce that asks nothing of you but patience — a love letter to the long simmer.

M
By Mira Delacroix Food Editor · 9 min read · June 15, 2026

There is a particular silence that settles over a kitchen at two in the afternoon, when the onions have gone soft and gold and the whole apartment smells of something that won't be ready for hours. I used to fear that silence. Now I chase it.

My grandmother never owned a timer. She judged her ragù by the way the surface of the sauce moved — a lazy blip, never a boil — and by the color, which shifted from bright tomato red to a deep, brooding brick over the course of an afternoon. "The sauce tells you," she'd say, waving a wooden spoon stained the color of autumn. For years I thought this was mysticism. It is, in fact, chemistry, and the most generous kind: it rewards anyone willing to do almost nothing for a very long time.

"Good ragù is not cooked. It is coaxed."

— Nonna Lucia, on the only rule that matters

The mistake most people make is heat. They want the sauce to hurry, so they crank the burner and stand over it, stirring anxiously, and what they get is something thin and sharp that tastes of impatience. A true Sunday ragù barely simmers. You set it, you crack a window, you go read on the couch. The collagen in the meat slowly surrenders; the tomatoes lose their acid edge; the wine cooks down into something round and dark. None of it can be rushed, and that is the entire point.

A pot, a window, an afternoon

I make mine in a wide enameled pot — the kind that holds heat like a grudge. The meat is browned in batches so it sears rather than steams, then set aside while the soffritto does its quiet work. Tomato paste goes in to caramelize until it smells almost like toffee. Then the wine, the crushed tomatoes, a parmesan rind, and the browned meat back home in the pot. After that, your only job is to leave it alone.

Somewhere around hour four the apartment changes. The smell stops being "cooking" and becomes a kind of weather, something you live inside. Neighbors text. People wander into the kitchen "just to check." This is the real recipe — not the quantities below, but the gravity a long-simmering pot exerts on a Sunday, pulling everyone toward the stove.

"You are not making dinner. You are making an afternoon."

When it's finally done — when the meat falls apart at the nudge of a spoon and the fat has risen into a glossy, garnet-colored slick — you fold it into wide ribbons of fresh pasta, shower it with parmesan, and eat it slowly, because by now you've learned how. The recipe is below. Take your time with it. That's the only ingredient that matters.

— M.D.

The Recipe

Slow Sunday Ragù

A low-and-slow meat sauce for fresh pasta. Mostly hands-off, deeply hands-on with patience.

  • Prep25 min
  • Cook6 hr
  • Serves6
  • LevelEasy-ish

Ingredients

  • 2 tbsp olive oil
  • 1 lb (450 g) ground beef chuck
  • ½ lb (225 g) ground pork
  • 1 onion, 1 carrot, 1 celery rib — finely diced
  • 3 cloves garlic, minced
  • 3 tbsp tomato paste
  • 1 cup (240 ml) dry red wine
  • 1 can (28 oz) crushed tomatoes 🍅
  • 1 parmesan rind + 1 bay leaf
  • ½ cup (120 ml) whole milk
  • Salt, pepper, a fistful of patience

Method

  1. Brown the beef and pork in batches over high heat; remove and set aside.
  2. Lower the heat. Soften the onion, carrot, celery and garlic until nearly melting, 12–15 min.
  3. Stir in tomato paste; cook until it darkens and smells sweet, ~3 min.
  4. Deglaze with wine; scrape the pot and reduce by half.
  5. Return the meat. Add crushed tomatoes, parmesan rind and bay. Bring to the barest simmer.
  6. Cover loosely and cook 5–6 hours, stirring now and then. It should blip, never boil.
  7. Stir in milk in the final 30 min. Season. Toss with fresh pasta and lots of parmesan.
🌿 Tip: make it a day ahead — it's even better reheated.
